# Daily AI Briefing: 2026-05-11

Four days since the last briefing, and the structural picture has
shifted. The NVIDIA print (May 20) is now nine days out, AMD's
post-earnings move has held, TSMC's five-fab 2nm ramp is on the
record, and the single biggest development since May 7 is
Microsoft's disclosure of an $80 billion Azure order backlog they
cannot fulfill because the electricity isn't there. Power, not
silicon, is now the binding constraint.

## Top moves since May 7

**NVIDIA.** Closed $207.83 on May 6 ahead of the May 20 print.
Consensus is $78.8B revenue and $1.77 EPS, in line with the
company's own guide. Jensen Huang publicly raised the demand
envelope from "$500B of high-confidence Blackwell + Rubin orders
through 2026" to "$1 trillion of opportunity through 2027."
Goldman Sachs consensus price target is $274.91, roughly 32% above
the May 6 close. Setup is high-expectation: a clean beat moves the
stock, an in-line print probably doesn't.

**AMD.** Q1 print on May 5 ripped the stock from $355.26 to
$430.15, a 21% one-day move that has held through the week. Up 66%
year-to-date, up 320% over twelve months. Morgan Stanley raised
its target from $255 to $360. The market is treating Helios plus
the Meta 6 GW deal as a real second source to NVIDIA, not a hedge.

**TSMC.** Five 2nm fabs in ramp simultaneously, the largest
expansion in company history. Monthly wafer output projected to go
from roughly 30-40K at end-2025 to around 100K by end-2026, a 70%
CAGR through 2028. Every 2nm wafer through 2026 is already booked
by Apple, Qualcomm, AMD, Google, and AWS. Advanced packaging
(CoWoS) capacity is growing 80%+ year-on-year, SoIC capacity 90%+.
TSMC has also said it will raise advanced-node prices for four
consecutive years.

**Microsoft.** Disclosed an **$80 billion Azure order backlog**
that cannot be fulfilled because of power constraints, not chip
availability. Satya Nadella stated GPUs sit idle in inventory
because the company lacks the electricity to install them. This is
the cleanest single data point in the cycle that the bottleneck
has moved off silicon.

**Frontier labs.** Anthropic released Claude Opus 4.7, the model
running this conversation, with material gains in multimodal
reasoning. Anthropic's Mythos Preview rolled out April 7 to eleven
cybersecurity organizations, capable of finding critical
vulnerabilities in operating systems and browsers. OpenAI shipped
GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.5 Pro. GPT-5.5 currently leads FrontierMath, the
hardest public math benchmark, at 51.7% on tiers 1-3 and 35.4% on
tier 4. Cadence remains roughly one frontier model release every
eleven days across the top labs.

## Key news

1. **The Microsoft $80B Azure backlog disclosure**, set against the
   IEA projection that global data center electricity consumption
   doubles to 945 TWh by 2030, is the structural story of the
   week.
2. **Combined 2026 hyperscaler capex** at Google, Amazon,
   Microsoft, and Meta is now estimated at $725B, up from the
   $690B figure on May 7 and 77% above 2025. That is consuming
   close to 100% of operating cash flows versus a 40% ten-year
   average. The buildout is now financed by debt at scale.
3. **Transformer lead times are 128 weeks.** Nearly two and a half
   years to order the electrical equipment that converts grid
   power into rack-usable voltage. Even if a hyperscaler signs a
   power contract today, the gear to deliver the electrons doesn't
   arrive until 2028.
4. **TSMC pricing.** Four consecutive years of price increases on
   advanced nodes, with 2026 capacity already fully booked. The
   question shifts to allocation: which customers get what share
   of 2027.

## Long-arc theme: the bottleneck moves to power

A year ago the AI bottleneck story was *chip supply*. Six months
ago it was *advanced packaging*, CoWoS and HBM. Today it has moved
one layer further down the stack to *electricity*. Microsoft has
the orders. They have, or can get, the chips. They cannot get the
megawatts to install them. The transformer that converts grid
power to rack-deliverable voltage is now a 128-week order item.
This matters because compute scaling laws have always quietly
assumed electrons are free and infinite. They are neither. The
next chapter of the buildout will be defined less by who designs
the best accelerator and more by who can build, contract, or
acquire the actual generating and grid capacity to feed it. This
is the *datacenter economics* and *power constraints* row of the
horizontal track in CLAUDE.md, and it is no longer a
forward-looking concern. It is the current chapter.

## Today's learning task

You locked `//` (floor division) and `%` (modulo) in session 2
today. The Microsoft backlog and the transformer lead time are
arithmetic problems you have the operators for. Predict each
output in your head, write the prediction down, then we run them
next Python session:

```
print(80_000_000_000 // 1_000_000_000)
print(128 // 52)
print(128 % 52)
print(945 // 365)
```

What each line is asking:
- Line 1: How many billions are in $80 billion? Sanity-check the
  operator on a number you already know the answer to. The
  underscores are just visual separators, Python ignores them.
- Line 2: How many full years is 128 weeks?
- Line 3: How many leftover weeks past the last full year?
- Line 4: 945 TWh per year, what is the per-day average in TWh?

Same operators as `10 // 4` and `11 % 3`, applied to the numbers
that actually describe the cycle. The bottleneck story isn't
abstract. It's a couple of divisions.
